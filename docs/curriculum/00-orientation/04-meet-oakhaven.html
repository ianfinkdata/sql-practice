4. Meet Oakhaven

Previous: 3. Tools and Setup. Next: Tier 1 — Beginner. Exercises: 4. Meet Oakhaven.

The company

Oakhaven is a fictional outdoor-gear retailer: think hiking boots, tents, climbing chalk, ski jackets, water bottles. It sells through two channels — online and in physical stores — has a roster of employees across sales, support, warehouse, and management, and has accumulated several years of order history from a customer base spread across the US.

None of that is real. But the database behind it is real, sitting right here at project/oakhaven.db, generated by a script with a fixed random seed so it's the same every time you build it. Every number and example in this course comes from actually querying that file — not invented, not approximated.

Oakhaven is deliberately small enough to hold in your head: five raw tables, a few hundred to a few thousand rows each. Small enough that you can SELECT * a whole table and skim it, but large enough that "just look at it" stops being a substitute for actually writing SQL. That's exactly the size where SQL starts paying for itself.

The five bronze tables, at a glance

"Bronze" is a name you'll see a lot — more on that in a moment. For now, just think of it as: the raw tables, as originally recorded. There are five:

TableWhat it holds
bronze_customersOne row per customer: name, contact info, state, signup date, account status, and customer segment (Retail/Wholesale/VIP).
bronze_productsOne row per product Oakhaven sells: name, category, brand, cost, price, and a SKU code.
bronze_employeesOne row per employee: name, department, region, hire date, and whether they're a manager.
bronze_salesOne row per order line — the transactional heart of the database. Each row is one product sold on one order: quantity, price at time of sale, discount, payment method, and more.
bronze_calendarOne row per calendar date, from 2018 through 2038. Not "messy" like the others — it's a manufactured reference table used for date-based reporting later in the course.
Notice the sizes are wildly different: ~600 customers, ~150 products, ~35 employees, but **12,000** rows of sales — because every customer can place many orders, and every order can have multiple line items. That's normal: transactional tables are almost always the biggest table in a retail-style database, and it's why so many SQL questions in the real world end up centering on a table like `bronze_sales`.

A first preview: bronze, silver, gold

You'll hear these three words constantly through this course, so here's the plain-English version now, without any SQL attached yet.

This pattern is called a medallion architecture, and it's a common way real companies organize a data pipeline into layers of increasing trust and polish:

  • Bronze — raw, as-received data. Messy on purpose: inconsistent
casing, missing values, mixed date formats, even outright bad data like negative costs or orphaned foreign keys. This is what you just looked at. Nobody "fixes" bronze — it's meant to preserve exactly what came in, mess and all, so nothing is silently lost or misrepresented.
  • Silver — cleaned and standardized. Same information, but names
are consistently cased, dates are parsed into one format, messy boolean text becomes real 0/1, and known data-entry bugs (like a discount stored as 25 instead of 0.25) get corrected. You'll meet this layer starting in Tier 2.
  • Gold — business-ready. Reshaped for actual reporting and
analysis: renamed into business-friendly terms, joined together, sometimes pre-aggregated (e.g. "total sales by month and category"). This is what a dashboard or report would actually query. You'll meet this layer in later tiers.

Every table you looked at above (bronze_customers, bronze_products, bronze_employees, bronze_sales, bronze_calendar) is bronze. The whole rest of this course is, in a sense, the story of turning these five raw tables into something trustworthy and useful — one SQL concept at a time. You don't need to remember any of the bronze/silver/gold details yet; just file away that the mess you saw above is intentional, and there's a plan for it.

Key takeaways

  • Oakhaven is a small, fictional outdoor-gear retailer with five raw
("bronze") tables: `bronze_customers`, `bronze_products`, `bronze_employees`, `bronze_sales`, `bronze_calendar`.
  • bronze_sales is the biggest table (12,000 rows) and the
transactional core — one row per order line, not per order.
  • Bronze data is intentionally messy — inconsistent casing, mixed
formats, missing values — because that's what real raw data looks like.
  • Bronze → silver → gold is a medallion architecture: raw → cleaned
→ business-ready. This whole course is organized around that one pipeline, applied to this one small database.
